Security Awareness

Practical ways to recognise phishing, whatever a tool says.

Checking a link

  • Read the domain from right to left. In paypal.com.account-check.xyz the real domain is account-check.xyz, not PayPal.
  • Watch for look-alikes such as paypa1, rnicrosoft or extra words like -secure-login.
  • Be wary of raw IP addresses, URL shorteners and long, random-looking addresses.
  • HTTPS (the padlock) only means the connection is encrypted – phishing sites use it too.
  • Hover before you click to see where a link really goes.

Checking an email

  • Urgency and threats ("within 24 hours", "account suspended") are designed to stop you thinking.
  • Requests for passwords, PINs or card numbers are a red flag – genuine organisations do not ask for these by email.
  • Check the full sender address, not just the display name.
  • Generic greetings such as "Dear Customer" can indicate a mass phishing campaign.
  • Unexpected attachments or prizes deserve extra caution.

If you are unsure

  • Contact the organisation using details you already trust (their official website or app, or the number on your card).
  • Do not reply, click links or open attachments in the suspicious message.
  • In the UK, forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk and suspicious texts to 7726.
  • If you entered details on a suspicious site, change your password straight away and contact your bank if payment details were involved.
